I. THEME
As education researchers, we find ourselves at a critical
inflection point—one marked by a growing public distrust of
research, political and governmental constraints on intellectual
inquiry, institutional attacks on academic freedom, and
fracturing within scholarly communities. Global in scope, these
myriad challenges raise urgent questions about the sanctity
and future of research and educational institutions. We are
charged to honor our capacities, as education researchers,
to co-create and facilitate knowledge that investigates
fundamental research questions while addressing pressing
societal concerns (Ladson-Billings, 2006).
At such moments of rupture, scholarship that foregrounds
our interrelationships, shared understanding, and collective
responsibility becomes especially needed. A growing body of
research conceptualizes learning as occurring within ecosystems
shaped by relationships, institutions, cultural histories, and
social practices (Lee, 2010; Nasir, 2024). From this perspective,
knowledge creation and human flourishing are sustained,
not in isolation or through individual achievement and formal
institutions. They occur through communal arrangements
marked by enduring social ties, shared moral commitments,
historical continuity, and cultural affirmation, particularly within
communities that have long been marginalized or dispossessed.

The theme of the 2027 AERA Annual Meeting, “Transcending
Borders and Boundaries in Education Research: Toward a
Beloved Global Community,” responds to the challenge of
the moment while serving as an invitation. The theme invites
scholars to learn from one another, communicate across
difference, and remain attentive to power and what is happening
within and beyond our diverse research spaces. It encourages
education researchers to reconceive research not merely
as a technical production, but as an ethical, equitable, and
relational practice that moves beyond nation-states, scholarly
disciplines and traditions, institutions, and epistemologies. We
are pleased that Toronto, Canada—an international city whose
demographics mirror that of our global education research
community—is hosting our 2027 annual meeting.
Transcending entails crossing conventional and established
boundaries with responsibility and accountability (Tate,
2012). It also requires engaging critically with institutional
and professional incentives, which too often reward the
maintenance of established boundaries and conformity over
intellectual risk-taking. The call is for sustained reflection on
how knowledge is conceptualized, authored, authorized, and
valued. The 2027 theme emphasizes AERA’s role as a bridge
that connects a diverse scholarly community, regardless
of researchers’ locations, preferred scholarly traditions,
or the institutions where they work. This theme focuses on

Across millennia, rigid ideas about humanity and learning
have produced artificial borders and boundaries, which
have narrowed ways of knowing, established hierarchical
domination, and fragmented communities. Building on the
2026 AERA theme by Maisha Winn, “Unforgetting Histories and
Imagining Futures,” the 2027 theme, “Transcending Borders
and Boundaries in Education Research,” also involves looking
to the past to imagine more humane and equitable futures.
The expansion of voices and epistemologies in the twentieth
and twenty-first centuries that challenged long-standing ideas
has reshaped our field; this theme urges us to deepen that
transformation for the present and future generations. As
education researchers, we have been able to hold empiricism
and emancipatory inquiry in creative tension (Du Bois,
1940/2017), and center dialogue, praxis, lived experiences,
and collective struggle (Freire, 1970). Moreover, Toni Morrison
(1987) implores us through her concept of the “beloved”; rather
than seeking consensus or closure, a beloved community must
grapple with the difficult work of remembering and repairing.

Education researchers are called to engage in principled dialogue
with one another and to be aware of the scholarly, policy, and
practice implications of their research. Grounded in traditions of
community-based scholarship and the moral vision of the Beloved
Community articulated by Howard Thurman and embraced by
Martin Luther King Jr., the theme urges transformative inquiry
rooted in humanity, open-ended engagement, care, meaning,
and ethical responsibility. It embodies the Kiswahili concept of
Ujima: collective work and responsibility.

The 2027 Annual Meeting in Toronto, Canada, welcomes
participation from scholars at all career stages and across
institutional locations, including community-based researchers,
school district personnel, graduate students, community
college faculty and staff, research institute fellows, and
university scholars. AERA is more than a venue for presenting
research findings; we are also a scholarly community—a
sanctuary that facilitates and protects intellectual exchange,
provides renewal, and promotes collective responsibility.
Amid ongoing global displacement, suppression of speech,
and the erasure of intellectual, practical, scientific, and sacred
ways of knowing, this theme affirms diversity, relationality,
mutuality, and communal bondedness (Morris, 1999, 2009).
When we invoke the “global,” we do so with the fullness of
humanity in mind, not as a hierarchy, but rather as an inherent
commitment to ethical interconnection. Research, in this
vision, is a co-creation among researchers and communities.
Attentive to histories of coloniality and dispossession, the
2027 theme reverberates with Smith’s (2021) reminder
that research, far from being value-free, must transcend
established boundaries and acknowledge the histories,
knowledges, and cultures of people that have been studied.

With these traditions and moral vision taken together,
“Transcending Borders and Boundaries in Education
Research: Toward a Beloved Global Community” provides
a more expansive and generative frame for AERA’s global
membership. It welcomes scholarship on migration, diasporic
education, sexuality and gender, disabilities, policy, ethnicity,
intersectionality, and beyond. And it insists that such work
remain attentive to power, accountability, and collective
flourishing. In centering relationality, ethical practice, and
imaginative possibility, the theme both reflects and extends a
long-standing scholarly tradition committed to using education
research to build more connected, humane, and hopeful worlds.
In the spirit of the transcendent scholar Howard Thurman’s
Beloved Community (1971), the 2027 AERA theme affirms that
community flourishes not through isolation, but through the
widening of its boundaries, through relationships with others
beyond ourselves. I invite you to join us in Toronto, Canada, as
we—literally, figuratively, and communally—transcend borders
and boundaries and renew our commitment to building the
Beloved Global Community.

Session Formats for Paper Submissions
When you submit an individual paper for presentation in
Toronto, you will be asked to select your preferred session
format. To submit, you select “place-based paper” submission.
You then select your preferred session format of paper, poster,
or roundtable. All formats provide a means for grouping related
papers into sessions.
•

Note that if you select “virtual paper,” you will not be
provided with an option to select a preferred session
format as accepted virtual papers will be presented only in
the AERA i-Presentation Gallery.

•

For the Annual Meeting, rooms for paper sessions will be
set up theater-style; poster sessions will use touchscreen
video monitors; and roundtable sessions will be set up
with multiple tables distanced in each room to allow for
interactive discussion.

The following session formats are used by program chairs to
group accepted papers.
Paper sessions. In paper sessions, authors present
abbreviated versions of their papers, followed by comments/
critique if there is a discussant, and audience discussion.
Paper sessions are in 90-minute time slots and have a chair;
discussant(s) are optional. Chairs and discussants for paper
sessions are identified by unit program chairs. A discussant
(or discussants) is included as part of a paper session only if
a discussant’s expertise can add to the understanding of the
papers. A typical structure for a session with four or five papers
allows approximately 5 minutes for the chair’s introduction to the
session, 10 minutes per presentation, 20 minutes of critique,
and 15 minutes of discussion. Session chairs may adjust the
timing based on the number of presentations and discussants
(where applicable) scheduled for the session. Individuals must
be attentive to the time allocation for presenting their work in
paper sessions. In the case of multiple-authored papers, more
than one person may present, but multiple presenters must
divide among the presenters the total time available to them.
They should take steps to ensure that including more than one
speaker does not detract from the overall presentation of the
work or infringe on the time allotments for other presentations.
Poster sessions. Poster sessions combine display of a
web-based presentation on a touchscreen video monitor
with the opportunity for individualized, informal discussion
of the research with attendees during a 90-minute session.
Papers accepted for a poster presentation will be grouped by

the program chair into appropriate poster sessions. Poster
presentations need to be created in the AERA i-Presentation
Gallery for monitor display by presenting author(s) at their
poster session and for viewing through the Gallery. This
interactive technology will permit the presenter to include
video, sound, text, narration, and charts.
Roundtable sessions. Roundtable sessions allow maximum
interaction among presenters and with attendees. Papers
accepted for a roundtable session will be grouped by the
program chair into tables with three-to-five papers per table,
clustered around shared interests. Each roundtable session
will have a designated chair, who is knowledgeable about
the research area, to facilitate interaction and participation.
Because the emphasis is on interaction, there will be no
discussants or formal presentation of papers as in a paper
session. Each roundtable session will be scheduled for
a 90-minute time slot. Because of the emphasis on brief
presentations and discussion, no audiovisual equipment or
a power source is provided at roundtable sessions. Authors
wishing to display brief information may do so from their own
tablet or laptop computer (if you plan to do so, be sure the
battery is charged).

Session Formats
A session submission provides opportunities, not afforded
by a single paper, to examine a specific education research
problem or topic from a variety of perspectives, engage in
intensive discussion, or draw on a wide range of experiences.
Organizers of sessions propose the topic, identify participants,
and describe the format to be used. The organizer must obtain
the consent of all participants before advancing a session
submission.
In general, session submissions include presenters from
different institutions or from different research groups or teams.

Session submissions may, however, include participants from
the same institution or from a single research group or team
under unusual circumstances. The rationale for focusing on
the work of a single group must be specified in the submission,
and reviewers are expected to address this element in their
evaluation. Furthermore, in the case of a session submission
that presents work from a single unit, there must be at least
one discussant who is external to the project, group, or team
included in the session submission to permit constructive
critique and commentary.
All accepted sessions are in 90-minute time slots, except for
off-site visits, which may vary in length.
Demonstrations/performances. Demonstration/performance
submissions are directed to using, showing, or illustrating a
particular technique, tool, or method to convey the value of the
approach for research or to illustrate or present understandings
or findings through such a method. Participants in
demonstration/performance sessions are expected to prepare
a brief commentary paper addressing what the performance or
demonstration is, what it is seeking to convey or illustrate, and
what has been learned or could be learned through that tool
or method. These papers may include information about, or
links to information about, demonstrations or performances.
Submissions for demonstration/performance may have a
maximum of 6 participants. Demonstration/performance
submissions are expected to include a chair; discussant(s)
are optional.
Off-site visits. Off-site visits offer participants site-specific
learning, such as observation of a school in session. This
format takes advantage of the unique attributes of the city
in which the Annual Meeting takes place and connects
researchers with concrete examples of relevant work. The
organizer arranges for a block of supervised and structured
time—as little as two hours or as much as a day—to be
spent at a site other than the meeting rooms (e.g., a school,
museum, science lab, or community agency) toward a goal
compatible with the Annual Meeting’s theme and content.
It is useful to protect a period of time for shared reflection
on the visit. Organizers of off-site visits need to work
with the AERA Meetings Department on implementation.
Fees covering costs are typically assessed. Off-site visit
submissions are expected to include a visit leader and/or
chair; discussant(s) are optional.
Structured poster sessions. This format provides for
overarching consideration of a research topic or issue
while permitting significant opportunity for visual and
graphic presentation and individualized discussion with
presenters. Submissions for structured poster sessions
must have a minimum of 6 and a maximum of 12
posters. There must be a chair who offers an introduction.
Discussant(s) are optional. Poster sessions can also
include brief oral presentations to the audience gathered
as a group. Much of the time is devoted to direct discussion
between attendees and poster presenters. We encourage
submission of sessions with poster presentations that
are conceptually linked in terms of education research

Symposia. A symposium provides an opportunity to

as discussion among the presenters and discussants,

Working group roundtables. A working group roundtable
encourages substantive exchange and interaction among
researchers working on a common set of research issues,
problems, or themes. Such roundtable submissions are
encouraged from organizers working in areas that are
cross-cutting where there are shared research problems or
issues that would benefit from cooperation and exchange,
or where researchers with complementary interests would
benefit from new, synergistic discussions. Working group
roundtable submissions are not eligible for consideration
if all the participants are at the same institution or already
involved in formal or ongoing research collaboration. The
working group roundtable submission is designed to bring
individual researchers or teams of researchers together
for discussion where there is potential for new substantive
gains and research communication or cooperation. All
presenters are expected to prepare a brief paper or
commentary paper that frames the issues or problems they
wish to advance or examine. Submissions for working group

includes a chair; there is no discussant, however, for such

hollow square. Attendees may sit at the table (as space
permits) or in chairs surrounding the table.
Workshops. A workshop provides an opportunity to
exchange information or work on a common problem,

project, or shared interest. Presentations are brief, allowing
adequate time for reflective discussion and interaction.
The workshop format is designed for sessions that provide
information relevant to the research process or other

presenting research to public audiences, integrating
diverse theories, using a particular research method or

work of research professionals. Workshop submissions are
expected to include a chair. (Submissions for courses and

Division A Program Co-Chairs: Bryan A. VanGronigen (chair),
University of Delaware, bvg@udel.edu; and Ranjini JohnBull
(co-chair), East Carolina University, johnbullr25@ecu.edu
The 2027 AERA Annual Meeting theme, “Transcending Borders
and Boundaries in Education Research: Toward a Beloved
Global Community,” invites scholars to look back, cross
traditional boundaries, and engage in meaningful dialogue to
build a truly loving and inclusive global community. This theme
is particularly salient for Division A’s call for submissions, as
the division has recently revised its submission sections and
associated foci for the first time in over a decade. As a program
planning committee, we took a "look back" to evaluate what
the division has historically foregrounded, backgrounded, or
omitted—and to critically consider who and what may have been
advantaged or disadvantaged by those previous structures.
Division A’s revised sections foreground several key
concepts. First, we believe that leaders and leadership within
administration and organization must be defined broadly
to be representative of the diverse individuals working in
today’s schools, school districts, after-school programs, and
non-profit organizations. Historically, our field has focused
heavily on the principalship, often treating "educational
leader" as synonymous with "principal." Today, however, we
recognize a vast array of formal and informal leaders—from
new paraprofessionals with program design expertise from a
prior career to district-level supervisors tasked with coaching
school-level administrators. These leaders and the leadership
they exercise, whether positional or in function, profoundly
influence children, educators, and communities, and their
work deserves scholarly visibility. Second, we assert that
the educational leaders needed today must lead with the
end in mind, which is the active promotion of justice, equity,
diversity, inclusion, and belonging. Division A is committed to
highlighting scholarship that demonstrates how leaders can
be equipped—and have concretely worked—to improve both
the conditions and the outcomes for their students, educators,
and communities.
Third, as the current political climate illustrates, educational
leaders are not immune to the direct and indirect influences
of policy and governmental bodies, nor the people and
movements that shape them. Today’s leaders are perhaps
more public facing than ever before, called to represent and
answer for their organizations in an increasingly polarized
public square. This politicized nature of leadership warrants
increased attention so that Division A can better help leaders
contend with, and even thrive within, such contexts.
Fourth, we must remember that educational leaders are,
first and foremost, human beings. Those leading today’s
schools, school districts, after-school programs, and non-profit
organizations deserve to live healthy, fulfilling lives. Yet, the
labor required—such as navigating politically charged meetings
or ensuring student food security—is often taxing and can
lead to burnout or turnover. By foregrounding the emotional,
physical, and professional well-being of the workforce, we
strengthen the entire educational system and, in turn, better
serve our children and educators.

Fifth, the theme of building a global community acknowledges
that leadership occurs across a vast spectrum of contexts
around the world. Division A recognizes the vital lessons to be
learned from these intranational, international, and diverse
settings, specifically regarding how leaders successfully
serve their unique populations. These global lessons about
leader practices feed into our sixth and final key concept:
the importance of developing leaders across the entire
professional continuum. With rising turnover rates, the need
for high-quality leadership is urgent. Consequently, we need to
expand our focus beyond traditional graduate-level licensure
to include a wider array of entry points into the educational
leadership profession—such as aspiring leader programs, inservice professional development experiences, and systems
for equitable leader hiring and evaluation. Developing the
educational leadership profession is more than a single
program; it is a constellation of opportunities that requires our
collective attention.
These key concepts reflect Division A’s evolution in spotlighting
the people and the nature of the labor defining today’s
educational landscape. As a scholarly community, we flourish
when we widen our boundaries, invite more voices to the
table, and build bridges between different conceptions of why
leaders lead and how they should be nurtured and supported
in their developmental journeys.
Division A seeks submissions that respond to these key concepts
through rigorous scholarly inquiry. How do leaders, in concrete
terms, enact equity and promote belonging? How do they
navigate political tensions while maintaining a focus on highquality education? How can leaders refine the organizations

Section 2: Policy and Governance Leadership

The work of K–12 school leaders is fundamentally shaped by
policy and politics. Every decision that district, building, and
teacher leaders make is enabled, constrained, or complicated
by the governance systems, legal frameworks, and political
dynamics that surround their schools and districts. Section
2 invites scholarship that takes this reality seriously, not as
background but as the substance of leadership itself. This
section seeks research that examines how leaders at all levels
of the K–12 system understand, navigate, interpret, and shape
the policy and political environments in which they practice.
This section occupies a distinctive space within Division A.
Section 2 focuses on leaders as political actors operating
within systems of formal authority and public governance.
This section also shares thematic terrain with Division L
(Educational Policy and Politics), but our distinct contribution
lies in centering leadership, such as how the people who lead
schools and districts experience, mediate, resist, and enact the
policies that reach them—and how governance relationships
shape what leaders can and cannot do.
This focus has grown more urgent. Leaders today confront
intensified political polarization in the communities they serve,
shifting school board compositions, legislative interventions
into curriculum and staffing, and evolving accountability
pressures that reshape their roles in real time. The 2027
AERA Annual Meeting Theme speaks directly to this terrain.
Governance is organized around borders and boundaries:
jurisdictional lines between levels of government, political
divides between competing visions for public education, and
structural boundaries separating those who design policy
from those who enact it. This year’s theme is grounded in
the beloved community tradition which raises the question:
what would it mean for governance structures themselves to
embody collective responsibility, democratic participation, and
responsiveness to the voices of communities that have been
historically excluded from educational decision-making?

Under the leadership of Vice President Roland Sintos Coloma,
Division B takes up this call with a deep commitment to
broadening who is seen, heard, and engaged in curriculum
studies. We understand curriculum not simply as content or

shaped by histories of colonialism, migration, resistance, and
community knowledge. To transcend borders is not to erase
difference, but to engage it with care, rigor, and accountability,
particularly in a moment marked by global inequities, neoliberal
constraints, and the uneven distribution of educational

We invite scholarship that pushes the field to think across

in the communities and ecologies that give curriculum its
meaning. We especially welcome work that foregrounds the

Brown, diasporic, differently abled, and sexual and gender
minority communities, as well as other historically marginalized
knowledge traditions. Division B is a space for your work. We
seek to learn from lived experiences, subaltern knowledge,
and community-rooted inquiry, and to support actionable
solidarities that extend beyond academic discourse.

•

the dignity, funds of knowledge, and communal bonds of
various communities?

•

How can curriculum studies move beyond established
hierarchies of knowledge by centering relational,
communal, joyful, and diasporic ways of knowing?

•

How do curricula travel, transform, and take shape across
borders, and with what consequences for learners and
communities?

•

How do we cultivate a beloved global community of

power relations and benefits marginalized communities?

•

How do we critically examine our own positionalities and
the boundaries we reproduce within the field?

advocacy, and responsibility while working within a praxis

sustain this work?

Use descriptors that characterize your submission and will

For instance, if your work is transdisciplinary in nature [e.g.,

Author and participant identification. Per AERA’s policy, all
paper and session submissions—including symposia—must be
submitted without author identification to allow for anonymized
review. In the case of session submissions, all participants

must be deidentified. Submissions that are not deidentified
will be removed from consideration.
Authors are responsible for removing any information from
their submissions that might lead a reviewer to discern their
identities or affiliations. References in the author line, within
the text (citations, footnotes, webpages, etc.), and within the
reference list for each author should be deidentified. Within
the text, mask self-citations of published work for each author
and for each citation with any author, regardless of authorship
order. Replace author names with “Author” in both in-text
citations and reference entries. For example, instead of “In our
previous analysis we found that… (Woods & Stone, 2004),”
write “In our analysis we found that… (Authors, 2004).” The
only exception to this rule is when deidentification would
not adequately decrease the likelihood that a reviewer
could infer authorship, such as when an author’s work
is prominent enough to be recognized regardless of
citation information (e.g., “My work on social cognitive
theory [Author, 2001]…”). In such cases, write in third
person to mask the work (e.g., “Bandura’s [2001] work on
social cognitive theory…”). Be sure active website links are
replaced with masked placeholders (e.g., PROJECT WEBSITE),
the identifying grant numbers are masked (e.g., PROJECT
NUMBER), and references in footnotes or tables are also
masked. Entries in the reference list for author self-citations
should be re-alphabetized under “A” for “Authors” rather than
leaving the entries in their original placement in the section.
Reference entries for “Author” should show only “Author” and
the year. Do not include article titles, DOIs, or other identifying
information. Note: This same guidance extrapolates to
session submissions wherein all participants need to
be anonymized.

Division G: Social Context of Education

In this moment of angst and anxiety, let us comprehend
the persecution of U.S. education as Howard Thurman saw
it—“a vote of confidence, which becomes, in turn, a source
of inspiration, power, and validation.” We recognize
the powerful nature of scientific research, theoretical and
conceptual explorations, and the immense impacts these
elements incur from educating the disinherited masses and the
privileged elite. Let us use these social and political assaults
as sources of inspiration to generate new and stronger
quality research, conceptual framing, and theoretical depth
that can be wielded to undertake institutional and structural
difficulties, guide the personnel whose various interpersonal
work sustains multitudes of academic pipelines, and reveal
the lived experiences of children, youth, adults, and families.
Our power is our scholarship often generated locally and
globally in the contexts of relations with schools, families and
communities. Our scholarly chorus is what drew the attention
of those who oppose racial and social justice, critical thought,
historical wisdom, and an inclusive vision of humanity. Now
is the time to ask and answer more complex questions that
illuminate a quickly (de)evolving era in education in which
the promise of an educated citizenry is under attack. We
invite the application of theoretical and methodological tools
that advance better understandings of the embattled social
contexts of education. Without formidable trade tools we enter
an embattled future unprepared and unprotected. And though
we first thought to speak of this metaphorically, the reality is
that we are at war for the equity, engagement, acceptance,
understanding, and the success of today and tomorrow’s
promise to curate an educated citizenry. Thurman poses the
question we seek to answer, “Under what terms is survival
possible?” Not only are we directed by this question, but we
expand it to include concepts of success, joy, solidarity, and
yes, love as aspects of resistance and resilience.
Lastly, we have already received validation for our efforts.
The field of education would not be under duress and extreme
suppressive tactics to silence scholarship, teaching, and
service if we were not making inroads and impacts in diverse
ways and innumerable areas of society. We would not be “living
rent free” in the heads of the powerful if laying bare the myriad
social factors that influence the educational experiences of the
“dispossessed” (Thurman) was ineffective.

communities and impact broader society.

annual meeting theme: “Transcending Borders and
Boundaries in Education Research: Toward a Beloved
Global Community.” Consistent with our teaching and teacher
education aims, we welcome scholarship that elevates and
expands our profession as “Architects Without Borders:
Educators Co-Constructing Beloved Community.” An
architect assumes many roles in building intentional structures,
including planning and design, project management, and
collaboration. As a community of teacher education researchers,
we are responsible for scholarship that positions teacher
educators and teachers as collaborators in the design and
delivery of learning experiences that optimize outcomes.
Currently, the critical role of teaching and teacher education
continues to be diminished through challenges such as
professionalization, standardization, and policy constraints
in many parts of the world. By operating in geographic (e.g.,
individual classrooms, districts, states, countries) and
ideological siloes (e.g., reading wars, routes to preparation,
school choice), artificial borders diminish our ability to develop
a cohesive—or complementary—vision for our field. The policy
structures, administrative guidance, preparation and practice
standards, and evaluation systems used to inform professional
learning and student growth must be responsively designed to
ensure the collective advancement of a productive citizenry.
Without intentional design and internal monitoring, our field is
easily defined and/or circumvented by external entities holding
power. As teacher education researchers, we must fortify our
foundation to address vulnerabilities and to withstand testing
over time (Sleeter, 2026).
Architecture is an act of intention, of vision, and of
consequence (Hollins & Warner, 2021; Hollins, 2026). We
contend that teacher education research is as well—through
the design of our research and contributions we make to
teaching and teacher education. Teacher educators who view
themselves as architects have envisioning, planning, and
design responsibilities. We can follow the same blueprint—
conducting research that maintains the status quo and
preparing teachers to function within the existing structures, to
manage its demands, to survive its pressures, to occasionally
subvert it in individual classrooms (White-Smith et al., 2018).
Or, we can engage in scholarship and practice that prepares
effective teachers and teacher educators to transcend social
and political boundaries. We can reframe the architecture and
co-construct new realities for current and future generations,
broadening our understanding of what counts as education
and education systems.
Scholars have documented that traditional schooling was
not built for all of our students; this is not an abstraction.
We are naming a structural reality: that the architecture of
public schooling in the United States, Canada, and much of
the world was designed within—and in some cases explicitly
to serve—colonial, racial, and class hierarchies (Smith, 2021;
Freire, 1970). Indigenous students, Black students, immigrant
students, students with disabilities, LGBTQ+ students, and
students from economically marginalized communities have
consistently encountered school buildings and systems not
designed to support their full humanity. And yet these same

communities have always carried with them knowledges,
practices, and ways of being that exceed and resist those
structures. It is precisely because of this history of survival,
ingenuity, and border-crossing that learning environments
are “ripe for traversing between spaces of freedom and the
classroom” (Jones & Howard, 2022). Teacher educators who
see themselves as architects must become builders of the
conditions in which all learners, drawing on the full richness of
their cultural and diasporic inheritances, are prepared to cross
borders of discipline, nation, and epistemology in service of
one another. Nowhere is this architectural responsibility more
urgent, or more contested, than in how we design, adopt, and
teach with technology.
In IT architecture, three primary goals drive the design of
systems: connection, cohesion, and changeability. As teacher
education scholars, we are most effective when we connect
within and across disciplines, levels, and communities. By
engaging with various key interest holders (e.g., families,
community members, policy makers) to develop and conduct
meaningful research, we are better situated to understand and
influence the learning ecosystem. Furthermore, having ongoing
connections with key interest holders allows us to adapt
and be responsive in a rapidly changing context (McCray &
Boveda, 2023). Applying architectural frameworks to systems
surrounding teaching and teacher education research requires
transdisciplinary and transnational perspectives.
Technology is neither neutral nor inevitable in design. Algorithms
privilege whiteness and actively discriminate enacting harm
on communities already marginalized by existing structures
(Noble, 2018). Emerging technologies can hide and accelerate
discrimination while appearing progressive, encoding inequity
into the very architecture of digital life (Benjamin, 2019).
That said, technology is also a site of diasporic resistance
and imagination. Equity in education is inseparable from the
material conditions technology in classrooms creates and from
who controls, designs, and imagines those conditions, including
the ability to engage with the language of futurity in ways that
create and sustain liberatory futures (Jones & Howard, 2022;
Winn, 2022). Architects without borders do not simply adopt the
tools they are given; they interrogate, redesign, and build anew.
Ultimately, teacher educators and teacher education
scholars must co-construct new sites of possibility or allow
our classrooms to serve as sites of reproduction to our own
detriment (Goodwin, 2026). We can prepare teachers to build
something better. This work must be done together—across
borders, across disciplines, across the academy and the
community—in the spirit of Beloved Community.
We are particularly interested in submissions that examine
the influences of teaching and teacher education at the
intersections of race, culture, language, class, gender, gender
identity, sexuality, dis/ability, and neurodiversity across
contexts. By engaging in transdisciplinary and transnational
dialogue, we can develop more holistic and inclusive
approaches that address the complex needs of learners
around the globe. We must continue to develop a more
cohesive knowledge base of teaching and teacher education

participants.

successfully planned, executed, and received.

The course title, abstract, proposal, and learning objectives
